    1.1 °CSINCE 1850–1900
    What it is

    Long-term shifts in temperatures and weather patterns. Today they are mainly caused by people [6].

    Why it happens

    Burning coal, oil and gas releases carbon dioxide (CO₂) and other gases. They act like a blanket around the Earth and trap the sun's heat [6].

    How much so far

    The world is already about 1.1 °C warmer than in 1850–1900, and scientists say it is clear that humans caused it [5].

    Why it matters here

    Every bit of extra warming makes heavy rain and droughts stronger in many places [5]. For the Yellow River, that means floods and droughts.

    Everyone has a part

    “Everyone must take climate action” [6]

    Governments, companies, communities and each of us.

    05 · What has already changed

    People reshaped the river; now the climate is shifting too

    Before looking ahead, what has already changed over the last few decades?

    People, more than climate (so far)

    >65%

    of the fall in river flow since the 1980s was caused by people: water taken for farms, cities and industry, plus terraces and new vegetation; climate caused the rest [19]

    226 days

    with no water reaching Lijin, near the river mouth, in 1997, mainly because water was taken without limits. Basin-wide water sharing began in March 1999 [20]

    −90%

    less mud (sediment) in the river than at its peak, thanks to check dams, terraces and replanting on the Loess Plateau [11]

    At the limit

    The Loess Plateau's new trees and grass already use nearly all the water local rain can support; planting more would cut the water left for people [21]

    A climate already on the move

    +0.66 °C

    per decade in the source region (1981–2015): about 2.3 °C in 35 years, twice the 1.1 °C the whole world has warmed since 1850–1900; frozen ground is thawing [5], [14]

    Drier

    Droughts have grown more severe across most of the basin since the 1950s, more and more because warmer air pulls water from soil and plants, not only because of less rain [22]

    Since 2000

    the biggest yearly flood peaks have been rising again, after falling since the 1840s, and extreme floods have become more frequent [23]

    2–5×

    the normal rain fell on the lower river in autumn 2021 (late Aug–Oct); the Yiluo and Qin rivers had their biggest inflow on record, bringing rare autumn floods [24]

    Takeaway

    So far people have shaped the river more than climate. Climate change now adds new pressure on top.

    05 · What has already changed

    Key moments: from a dry river to new floods

    1997

    No water reached Lijin, near the sea, for 226 days, mainly because water was taken without limits [20]

    1999

    1 March: first basin-wide water allocation order by the Yellow River Conservancy Commission; the river flows again ten days later [20]

    2000

    Xiaolangdi dam starts operating; since then the lower river's flow has been fully regulated by the dam [18]

    2016

    A study finds Loess Plateau greening close to the water limit: planting more would cut the water left for people [21]

    2021

    Late Aug–Oct: rain 2–5× normal on the lower river and record inflow from the Yiluo and Qin rivers bring rare autumn floods [24]

    2023

    1 April: the Yellow River Protection Law takes effect, with rules for water limits, ecological protection and flood and drought control [25]

    2026

    August: 27 years in a row without the river drying up, thanks to unified water management [26]

    06 · How we look at the future

    Why do scientists need computer models?

    We have seen how the river changed so far. But how can anyone study a future that hasn't happened yet?

    We only have one Earth

    We can't heat up the real planet just to see what happens. A model is a safe copy of the Earth that we can experiment on.

    We can't wait until 2100

    By the time we could measure the future, it would be too late to prepare. Models let us look decades ahead, today.

    Our choices change the future

    A model can run the same world on different 'roads' (low or high emissions) and show how much our choices matter.

    Think of a flight simulator: pilots practise flying through storms safely before they ever meet a real one. Climate models let us 'practise' the future in the same way.

    Observed

    measured in the past by instruments, like rain gauges and river stations

    Projected

    what models show could happen on one road: a 'what-if', not a forecast

    Many models

    studies run many models and report the middle value (median) and the range from lowest to highest

    Takeaway

    Models don't say exactly what will happen. They show what could happen on each road, so we can prepare in time.

    06 · How we look at the future

    Four steps from computer to river

    Each step uses a different kind of model, and each one adds something the step before cannot do.

    1 · Climate models

    A climate model is a computer copy of the whole Earth (air, oceans, ice and land) that follows the laws of physics. Scientists run it once for each road [5].

    Why it helps Shows how the whole planet heats up on each road.

    e.g. 23 global models were tested for this basin [1]

    2 · Make it fit our river

    Global models see the Earth in big squares, often 100 km or more wide: too blurry for one valley. Scientists zoom in on the basin (downscaling) and fix known errors with real rain records (bias correction) [13].

    Why it helps Turns blurry global results into rain and temperature we can trust, area by area.

    e.g. rain and temperature for each part of the basin

    3 · River models

    A river (hydrological) model works like a water budget: rain in, minus water that evaporates or soaks into the ground, gives the water flowing down the river, day by day [2].

    Why it helps Tells us how much water the river will carry, and when.

    e.g. natural river flow above Huayuankou

    4 · Count the extremes

    From many years of simulated river flow, scientists count how often floods and droughts happen and how big they are [2].

    Why it helps Turns long records into simple numbers, like 'once every 9 years'.

    e.g. how often today's 30-year flood comes back

    Takeaway

    Every future number on this site came through these four steps: a 'what-if' for one road, not a prediction.

    2 · What is an SSP? (In easy words)

    What it is

    SSP means Shared Socioeconomic Pathway: a 'what-if' story about the future world. How many people? How rich? Coal or sun for energy? Do countries work together?

    What the number means

    The number (2.6, 4.5 or 8.5) is how much extra heat the greenhouse gases trap by 2100, in watts per square metre. Bigger number = hotter world [27].

    What it is not

    Not a forecast and not a model. It is the input story that climate models run with, so that different studies can be compared [5].

    09 · Droughts in a wetter world

    More rain, yet worse droughts: the paradox

    Floods are only half of the story. Why can droughts get worse in a wetter climate?

    1 · Hotter air is thirstier

    Warming pulls water out of soil, crops and rivers faster. This already drives drying across most of the basin [22].

    2 · Rain comes in bursts

    More rain falls in short, heavy bursts. It runs off the hard ground into rivers and drains before the soil can soak it up, and the dry gaps in between get longer.

    3 · Rain at the wrong time

    When rain falls matters as much as how much. Extra water that comes in winter or in one big flood does not help crops short of water in July–August, when river droughts are becoming more common [2].

    Takeaway

    The same warming that makes floods bigger also makes droughts worse, so both extremes must be managed together.

    A drought starts in the sky but ends up in fields, rivers and taps. Each step takes time.

    How sure are we?

    Fairly confidenttap to see the studies
    • The basin keeps warming, fastest on the fossil road [28]
    • Heavy rain gets heavier [1], [5]
    • Flood risk rises in the middle and lower reaches [3], [16], [35]
    • Hotter air adds drought stress [13], [22]
    • Water stays scarce per person [2], [32]
    Less suretap to see the studies
    • Exactly how much rain each area gets [13], [28]
    • How big future floods will be: models range from small falls to more than double [2]
    • How fast cities and water use change [11], [19]
    • How thawing frozen ground changes river flow [14]
    • How often sudden swings will happen in future [4]

    Limits of our review

    • We reviewed published studies; we did not run new models.
    • Studies use different models, periods and baselines.
    • Some headline numbers come from a single study.
    • The action plan is our own proposal.

    Disclaimer · Why some dashboard cards say 'Not reported'

    Flood risk: the only study of rainstorm-flood risk on the lower river [3] gives values for the middle and fossil roads in 2021–2040 and 2081–2100 only. Another study [35] covers all three roads, but only to 2045 and with a different measure, so it cannot fill the gaps.

    Heavy rain: our basin study [1] reports 2021–2050 only. We found no other peer-reviewed, basin-wide study with the same measure for the other roads and periods (searched November 2026). Rather than mix in numbers that are not comparable, these cards stay empty.

    13 · Action plan

    Two kinds of action: turn down the tap, and get ready

    Knowing the risks is only useful if it leads to action. What can be done?

    Mitigation

    Cut the greenhouse gases that cause warming. The nine basin provinces emitted ~43% of China's CO₂ in 2017 [36].

    Adaptation

    Prepare for the change already on its way: floods, droughts, water shortage [5].

    The plan builds on existing policy: the 2021 basin outline plan [7], the Yellow River Protection Law [25], China's adaptation strategy to 2035 [37], and the goals to peak emissions before 2030 and reach carbon neutrality before 2060 [38].

    First step 1 of 6 · Pillar B

    Updated flood design guidance

    What it means

    Build dams, levees and drains for the bigger floods that are coming, not only for the floods of the past.

    Why now

    Floods get bigger and more frequent [2].

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    First step 2 of 6 · Pillar B

    Early warnings for long rainstorms

    What it means

    When a long, heavy storm is on its way, every village gets the warning in time and knows where to go.

    Why now

    A dry spell can flip to a wet one in 1–2 days [4].

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    First step 3 of 6 · Pillar B

    Safety check of check dams

    What it means

    Inspect the small dams that trap mud on the Loess Plateau, so they don't break one after another in a big storm.

    Why now

    A chain of check dams has failed before [34].

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    First step 4 of 6 · Pillar D

    Drought plans with trigger levels

    What it means

    Agree in advance what to do when water runs low: at which level to start saving, and who gets water first.

    Why now

    River droughts move into July–August [2].

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    First step 5 of 6 · Pillar C

    Keep to the water limits, openly

    What it means

    Each province has a water limit. Publish how much each one uses, so everyone can see the limits are kept.

    Why now

    Water stays below the scarcity line [2].

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    First step 6 of 6 · Pillar F

    Open data for everyone

    What it means

    Put rain, river, soil-moisture and groundwater data online for free, for cities, scientists and the public.

    Why now

    Good decisions need good data (Pillar F).

    First steps proposed by the authors.

    Takeaway

    No-regret first: saving water, early warning and open data pay off on every road.
