A visual field guide to the nucleus

Understand nuclear science, one half-life at a time.

Nuclear energy powers about a tenth of the world's electricity, treats cancer, dates fossils, and pushes rovers across Mars. This is a plain-language, well-sourced starting point — how atoms store energy, how reactors and radiation actually work, and where it all shows up in daily life.

01 — Fundamentals

Where nuclear energy actually lives

Every atom has a nucleus at its center, made of protons and neutrons bound together by the strongest force in nature. Nuclear energy is what gets released when that binding changes — a nucleus splits apart, or two nuclei fuse together.

Proton Positive charge. Its count defines the element — one proton is always hydrogen, 92 is always uranium.
Neutron No charge. Helps hold the nucleus together. Same element with different neutron counts = an isotope.
Electron Orbits the nucleus, governs chemistry — but plays almost no role in nuclear reactions.

Some isotopes are stable forever. Others are radioactive — their nucleus is slightly unbalanced, so it eventually rearranges itself and releases energy as radiation. That's the entire premise of nuclear science: energy stored in the nucleus, released on its own schedule or on ours.

02 — Two ways to release nuclear energy

Fission splits. Fusion joins.

Both convert a small amount of mass into a large amount of energy, following Einstein's E=mc². Every power reactor on Earth today runs on fission; fusion is what powers the sun, and is still being engineered into a practical power source here.

splitting heavy atoms

Fission

A heavy nucleus — usually uranium-235 or plutonium-239 — absorbs a neutron, becomes unstable, and splits into two smaller nuclei. This releases energy, radiation, and two or three new neutrons, which can go on to split further nuclei. Controlled carefully, that chain reaction is what keeps a power reactor running at a steady output.

joining light atoms

Fusion

Two light nuclei — typically hydrogen isotopes deuterium and tritium — are forced together under extreme heat and pressure until they merge into helium, releasing far more energy per reaction than fission. It's the reaction that powers every star. On Earth, experimental facilities like ITER and the National Ignition Facility are working toward sustained, net-positive fusion power.

03 — From atoms to electricity

How a nuclear power plant works

Most reactors are, at heart, a very sophisticated way of boiling water. The nuclear reaction is just the heat source.

01

Fission heats water

Control rods (absorbing neutrons) set the reaction rate; water both cools the core and slows neutrons so fission can continue.

02

Heat makes steam

In the design shown, heat passes through a steam generator into a separate loop of water, keeping reactor water contained.

03

Steam spins a turbine

High-pressure steam pushes turbine blades — the same principle as a coal or gas plant from this point on.

04

Turbine turns a generator

The spinning shaft turns magnets inside coils of wire, inducing electric current.

05

Steam is condensed

Used steam is cooled back into water using river, sea, or cooling-tower water, and returned to the loop.

06

Electricity reaches the grid

Transformers step up the voltage for efficient, long-distance transmission.

04 — What radiation is, and isn't

Radiation, shielded in context

"Radiation" just means energy traveling as particles or waves. Different types are stopped by different things — and everyone is exposed to some amount of it, every day, from entirely natural sources.

Alpha (α)

Heavy and slow. Stopped by a sheet of paper or the outer layer of skin. Dangerous mainly if inhaled or swallowed.

Beta (β)

Fast electrons. Passes through paper, stopped by a few millimeters of plastic or aluminum.

Gamma (γ)

High-energy light. Needs thick lead or concrete to meaningfully block it.

Neutron

Produced inside reactors. Best slowed by water, concrete, or other hydrogen-rich material.

06 — Beyond the power plant

Where nuclear science shows up in daily life

Roughly 90% of what nuclear science touches has nothing to do with electricity generation.

Medicine

Diagnosis & treatment

Short-lived tracers light up organs in PET and SPECT scans; targeted radiation shrinks tumors in radiotherapy and brachytherapy.

Agriculture

Food & pest control

Irradiation extends shelf life and kills pathogens; the sterile insect technique controls pests without broad pesticide use.

Industry

Seeing the unseen

Radiography inspects welds and castings for hidden flaws; smoke detectors use a tiny americium-241 source to sense particles.

Space

Powering deep space

Radioisotope generators convert the heat of decay directly into electricity, powering Voyager, Curiosity, and Perseverance far from the sun.

Archaeology

Dating the past

Carbon-14's steady, known decay rate lets scientists date organic material up to roughly 50,000 years old.

Energy

Low-carbon baseload

Fission plants run day and night regardless of weather, supplying a steady share of low-carbon electricity on the grid.

Research

Probing matter

Neutron beams from research reactors reveal the atomic structure of everything from proteins to jet-engine alloys.

Water

Desalination & tracing

Isotope tracing maps groundwater flow and leak points; reactor heat can also help desalinate seawater in some designs.

07 — Managing the risk

Safety is layered, on purpose

Reactor design follows "defense in depth" — several independent barriers, so that no single failure leads to a release of radioactive material.

01
Fuel ceramic pelletsUranium is formed into ceramic pellets that trap most fission products inside their crystal structure.
02
Fuel rod claddingA metal tube seals each stack of pellets, keeping fission products out of the cooling water.
03
Reactor vesselA thick steel vessel contains the coolant loop and reaction under high pressure.
04
Containment buildingA reinforced concrete and steel structure is the final barrier between the reactor and the outside world.

Used fuel doesn't disappear — it's managed

Spent fuel first cools in on-site water pools for years, then commonly moves to sealed dry casks for long-term, monitored storage. Countries including Finland and Sweden are building deep geological repositories designed to isolate high-level waste for the very long timescales it takes to decay to safe levels.

08 — Common questions

Quick answers

Is nuclear power the same thing as a nuclear weapon?

No. Power reactors use fuel enriched to roughly 3–5% uranium-235 and are physically incapable of exploding like a weapon, which requires very different materials and design. The chain reaction in a reactor is also deliberately kept slow and controlled.

How long does nuclear waste stay dangerous?

It depends on the isotope. Some fission products lose most of their radioactivity within decades; others take thousands of years, which is why long-term geological storage is designed around such long timescales.

Is living near a nuclear plant dangerous?

Under normal operation, a nuclear plant releases far less radiation into the surrounding area than natural background sources like radon or cosmic rays. Regulatory limits keep routine emissions to a small fraction of typical annual background dose.

Why do some countries rely on nuclear power heavily and others don't?

Choices depend on available fuel, upfront construction cost, existing grid infrastructure, public opinion after historical accidents, and competing energy priorities — it's more a policy and economics question than a purely technical one.
